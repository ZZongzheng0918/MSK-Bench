Abstract

Musculoskeletal (MSK) humanoids provide a physiologically grounded embodiment for studying full-body motor control, but their high-dimensional muscle actuation, delayed activation dynamics, and redundant muscle-tendon structures make learning substantially harder than torque-driven humanoid control. Existing MSK benchmarks remain fragmented across gait, prosthetics, dexterous hands, or challenge-specific tracks, leaving full-body muscle-actuated control insufficiently evaluated under standardized tasks, methods, and metrics. We introduce MSK-Bench, a benchmark of 22 full-body motor-control tasks organized into three progressively challenging categories: postural stabilization, common locomotor behaviors, and contact-rich environmental interaction. Under unified task protocols and robustness perturbations, MSK-Bench evaluates 5 representative control paradigms, including reward-based RL, agentic reward tuning, latent-action RL, imitation-prior control, and residual adaptation over imitation priors. Beyond task success and reward, MSK-Bench further reports robustness analysis and physiology-oriented diagnostics, including activation cost, joint smoothness, and walking EMG-envelope similarity. Our empirical study shows that embodiment-aware exploration improves task coverage in high-dimensional muscle spaces, imitation priors enhance reference-compatible stabilization and locomotion but degrade under contact-rich terrain mismatch, and residual adaptation can recover successful behaviors when fixed references fail. MSK-Bench provides a task-method-metric testbed for full-body muscle-actuated humanoid control.

Task Families

From postural regulation to contact-rich environmental interaction.

T1

Embodiment-aware exploration and action structure are essential for broad task coverage.

Full-body MSK control is difficult because many muscles act on fewer mechanical degrees of freedom, agonist-antagonist activations can cancel, and short uncoordinated commands are filtered by muscle activation dynamics before producing useful motion. Under the fixed training budget, DepRL achieves the broadest coverage by exploiting embodiment-aware sensor-action couplings, while DynSyn-SAC is competitive on action-structured skills such as sidestepping. The negative results are equally important: unconstrained online reward retuning makes the objective unstable, and aggressive action compression below moderate dimensions removes the bandwidth needed to coordinate posture, locomotion, and contact-rich behavior.

T2

Motion priors improve reference-compatible movement, but contact-rich terrain exposes tracking mismatch.

Imitation-prior control reduces exploration burden by supplying phase-structured gait and posture regularities, which helps on flat-ground and reference-compatible stabilization or locomotion tasks. However, once terrain height, contact timing, or object interaction deviates from the reference distribution, tracking errors accumulate. On stairs, for example, misplaced contacts and off-timing force generation make pure imitation brittle, whereas reward-driven exploration and residual adaptation can recover task-specific contact strategies.

T3

Physiology-oriented diagnostics reveal what task success alone hides.

Success rate and reward do not explain whether the controller moves in a biomechanically plausible way. Joint-smoothness analysis shows that jerk is shaped by the physical impact profile of each task: stabilization is smoothest, locomotion is highest-impact, and interaction falls between them. EMG-envelope comparisons show that task optimization can recover more human-like recruitment than imitation alone for some muscles. Load and anatomy studies further show that heavier powerlift weights shift activation toward trunk/back support, and the 700-muscle model recruits additional axial stabilizers that the 416-muscle model cannot express.

Residual Adaptation

Residual RL preserves imitation structure while learning task-specific muscle corrections.
